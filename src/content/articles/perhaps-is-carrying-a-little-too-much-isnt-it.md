## Table of contents

## Prelude

There is a particular genre of argument that does not defeat a question, but diagnoses the desire to ask it, describes the scenery around it, then walks off as though the investigation were done.

An excellent division of labor. The questioner provides the question, the philosopher provides the illness, and nobody has to provide the proof.

I ran into that maneuver time and again when somebody asked if the things called games had anything in common. Family resemblance would always get there before the candidate did. Eventually the phrase stopped annoying me, but how confidently it came to me did.

I wasn't sure if I was cross with Wittgenstein or with the little Wittgensteins who appear whenever somebody tries to define anything. So I followed up on the area: Philosophical Investigations[^pi-prelude-games], its remarks on exactness and the conception of philosophy[^pi-prelude-philosophy], and the previous diagnosis of a "craving for generality" in The Blue Book[^blue-prelude].

I have no philosophical team to defend here. At best, I lean toward Existential, some part of Sartre, some of Camus, but non of all, as I do not believe in human's nature, yet. That tells me something in advance of what games, numbers, laws, or other objects have in common. A temperament isn't a proof, either.

It made the position less easy to reject, and an objection that survives only until the target is read deserved to be dropped before the target is gone.

## Then I read what he was actually doing

The question in section 65 does not drop from the heavens. Wittgenstein has been describing many language-games and an interlocutor points out that he has never said what is common to them, what makes them language or parts of it. The objection demands the general form. It demands essence.

Wittgenstein says these activities have nothing in common that explains their sharing of a name, only various relations. Now comes the request not to think but to look. Board games, card games, ball games, winning, losing, skill, luck, amusement, competition: each likeness fails somewhere while another enters. The result is what he calls "family resemblances", a bundle of overlapping fibers, not one stretching from end to another. [^pi-family-resemblance]

And he even forecloses one obvious reply. Someone may point out that the constructions share a disjunction gathered from their overlapping properties, or that overlapping continuity per se runs through the strand. Wittgenstein calls this "playing with words". Presumably it is, if the postulated commonalty merely repeats an extension and explains nothing. But that observation already takes on what common is supposed to do. Not being useful for a definition does not annihilate it as a relationship, and not calling it wordplay does not offset the elimination of a distinction.

By section 69, explaining the ordinary word "game" no longer requires an analytic boundary. We show examples and say that similar things go there. Wittgenstein also denies the charge that a concept must be strictly bounded to be a concept at all. [^pi-sections-70-71] A sample becomes general through use, and a boundary drawn later produces one variety of a similar concept rather than finding one buried in ordinary use.

Reading it in its place, this is not a lazy effort at taxonomy. Wittgenstein is fighting a picture of meaning, which is himself suggests that a general word must be unified by a single common property, that understanding the word is having that property, and that a vague boundary demonstrates a deficient concept. His counter is that the use of a word can be ordered, teachable, and entirely serviceable without an analytic definition to stand behind it.

This is a point worth making. A hazy boundary is not the same as no use. A rule can be sufficient for one purpose without determining all edge cases. We can know how to proceed in familiar circumstances while being unable to produce a formula that mechanically determines every subsequent use. Ordinary language works without waiting through philosophy's paperwork to permit speech.

As long as the issue is this much, I hardly have the patience to disagree with him.

Heather Gert argues that the family resemblance presents fallible evidence for membership in a different extension, but not for membership as such. [^gert] That reading spares Wittgenstein an algorithm for membership, but constrains the extent of what the metaphor establishes. Evidence can guide but not prove that nothing else is common. Constitutional readings owe an explanation of the resemblance relation, Gert's evidential one escapes that obligation but achieves no ontological standing. Selected resemblances still cannot close the inquiry.

## The stronger it gets, the more it owes

Family resemblance is barely a piece of a broader philosophical method. The Blue Book made that impossible to miss.

The "craving for generality" aggregates several inclinations: looking for something common to all instances of a general term, supposing a mental picture underlying our grasp of it, and confusing the hypothetical mechanism for a state of consciousness. Most irritating to me, however, is how strongly the method of science intrudes: reducing the phenomena to a few laws and aggregating cases in generalization. Philosophy seduced by that method walks into darkness: it turns out to be "purely descriptive".[^blue-craving]

Philosophy, he says later, is "as we use the word", a battle of the fascination of forms of expression. The old title seems both apt and misleading, as though his subject were the child and not the uncontested proprietor of philosophy. That is a local description of Wittgenstein's pursuit and a warning about the name's inheritance. [^blue-as-we-use]

The target of his concern recurs as one of the sublime pureness formerly requested of logic, including the picture drawn in the Tractatus: we predicate what our method of representing a thing puts there, then mistake the ease of comparison for a condition of things in a most generalized form. A stronger statement for why method tends not to disclose its field of vision as reality is difficult to find. [^pi-section-104]

Philosophy is precluded from theories, explanation gives way to description and the actual use of language is allowed to stay as it is. The relevant facts are laid bare to sight, but familiarity causes them to be difficult to notice. [^pi-sections-109-129] There is room for philosophy to speak about its own name. Language-games, turn out to be comparisons, models measure like measuring rods, with various therapies serve different purposes. There is no one method, what philosophy desires is its peaceful discovery. [^pi-sections-130-133]

Therefore method examines itself. It speaks about its procedure, its intent, the danger of its models, and even the confusion of representation with reality. Unless, there is its competence. A method can regard itself in a mirror without understanding which wavelengths it was never exposed to.

Philosophy has become a clinic and that is fine, as long as somebody remembers that diagnosis of the patient remains a claim on the patient.

The most powerful response from a Wittgensteinian was plain to see: demanding a scientific proof from this project is not to understand it. Family resemblance is not a competitor to a scientific theory of games' hidden ontology, but a part of a grammatical inquiry into how words work and how philosophy gets itself tied up by a false demand made of them. "Look and see" is not an induction masquerading as a theorem, but therapy for the itch to manufacture one.

That response satisfies, which is inconvenient in the most useful way, but it draws the conclusion I need.

Rejecting scientific method does not reject a responsibility to prove what that method claims. The proof need not be a scientific experiment, an induction, or a formal derivation. The grammatical investigation may establish a grammatical conclusion by displaying relevant uses and the resulting confusion. The therapy may establish that a certain question arises from a certain picture by exposing the reliance. But as soon as either asserts that the question is illegitimate, that no explanation fits there, or that inquiry should stop, the conclusion must follow from something.

Calling these tools non-scientific changes the currency, but the debt is still on the ledger.

If family resemblance describes how the ordinary word "game" is learned, extended, and negotiated, it may show that a speaker does not check a necessary-and-sufficient definition. It may show that such a definition is not represented in their ordinary competence. It may show that the absence of it does not stop the use in its success. Those are valuable results about grammar and practice.

None of this determines what the objects themselves have in common. A grammar of recognition is not yet an account of the recognized object, a report about a word's use is not yet a result about what the word is applied to in all its variety. As long as either method sits on its own side of that border, my objection does not touch it and neither can it touch my question.

There is a limit to how far I can personally carry that charge: Wittgenstein died in 1951 and Philosophical Investigations appeared in 1953 after he withdrew the manuscript but allowed its posthumous publication. Part I was nearly a completed typescript in 1945-46, not a stack of notes from strangers. [^erbacher-edition] Still, he never saw the book, the quarrels, and the portable versions of his name glued into arguments decades later.

Some line items remain his property: the lack of any single thing in common, the disappearance of explanation, the descriptive limitation of philosophy, and the promise of the complete disappearance of its problems. The text does not fulfill all of those debts, and later readers owe their additions on removing the fine print. But the initial account was already considerable, without forging his name onto the remaining ones.

The interpreters still have no reliable uniform to parse these sentences. Someone finds theses[^horwich-reading], someone else finds therapy[^pichler-reading], some see a constructive philosophy[^pukhaev-reading] , and others locate an interventionist rather than a quietist Wittgenstein,[^moyal-sharrock-reading] so that the authority of the metaphilosophy remains in question.

Since I now knew the dispute was not one of description versus science, but of jurisdiction, I let the word "craving" linger on the page for a while to see where the charge ended up after it abandoned the book.

## Then the passport stopped matching the jurisdiction

Having separated the question of ordinary use and the question of what may be actually common makes the troubling step nearly embarrassing in its insignificance.

The text now offers a pretty decent bargain. It has earned its weight by being modest, so ordinary competence does not require a complete definition, ambiguous boundaries need not destroy a concept, and description breaks one false picture's hold. Family resemblance provides the suggestion of where to look and what to withhold of preestablished ideas. It does not decide what the objects have in common, forbid explanation elsewhere, or transform any search for commonality into a symptom. Several grander conclusions shrink, but everything remaining is valuable and comes with compensation.

The problem is that the phrase "philosophy only describes" never clears up what kind of sentence it is. As a description of what actual philosophers do it requires evidence and runs into considerable explanation found on the shelves. As a stipulation for what Wittgenstein calls philosophy it is coherent but limited. As a recommendation it requires a goal and a reason this method serves that purpose better. As a claim about the nature of philosophy it requires the metaphilosophy to reach that conclusion. As therapy performed on the reader it may demonstrate a cure for the wrong disease in one case without a medical authorization for all the others' inquiries.

The aspiration is not borrowed from later readers alone, since Wittgenstein himself says there is no one thing common to explain the shared word, explanation must disappear, philosophy can only describe, and its problems should disappear completely. A modest interpretation may limit those statements to the grammatical troubles being treated, but the limitation is an interpretation of language that already stretches. Taken by their own weight, the sentences have enough authority to close a road and take on all the burdens of the passage. The safer interpretation denies the injunction, and the prohibitive wording denies the safety.

This is the charge I cannot shake: the method comes as one measuring rod among many, carrying one order for one purpose that promises not to demand conformity from reality. A few pages before and after the border may not theorize, explanation must disappear, everything relevant lies bare to view, and the problems are expected to vanish. Jurisdiction extends far beyond the passport Wittgenstein handed to visitors.

Ordinary games appear extremely different. Ordinary surface properties prove themselves useless one after another. Now comes the modal phrase: perhaps there is no common essence. Soon enough the phrase gains a methodological consequence: stop looking for anything common, stop demanding a definition, stop accepting generality as a possibility of understanding.

That is a great deal of effort for perhaps.

The first overstatement arrives before essence, inside the phrase "nothing common". Common in what way, by what level, and under what relation? The word remains untyped while the inspection proceeds through a short list of familiar properties and delivers a judgment over the entire space of possible candidates.

Seeing across a collection defeats every candidate that is actually examined, since a counterexample removes the candidate it opposes, repeating the same procedure only adds more failed candidates and does not examine every candidate there can be. Amusement, competition, skill, luck, winning, and losing may all fail without carrying relations, conventions, abstractions, and higher-order properties with them. The variety within one inventory is not a theorem of impossibility over all kinds of commonality.

There are two ways to interpret the sentence "there may be no common essence". If it merely indicates a logical possibility, then indeed there may not be one. There also may be. Enumerating either possibility establishes no ordering between them nor allows any rule for inquiry. If the statement is a conclusion based on the survey of games, it owes the inference from failed candidates it lacks to the absence of an essence.

The sentence is harmless under the first interpretation and consequential under the second: it cannot draw the safety of the former while challenged, or claim the authority of the latter while continuing the investigation. No conclusion about essence arrives on credit.

Perhaps enters the room as a hedge and leaves with a court order, without anyone checking its pocket along the way.

A convenient escape from the bargain is to refuse the burden outright: perhaps philosophy is therapy rather than theory, perhaps asking for proof is merely a repetition of confusion, and perhaps the method has vindicated itself when the questioner loses the need to ask. But that is not a third statement, it is the ambitious one preserving its authority while returning the bill.

In the shadow of the return lies one very large question: why? Why does this diagnosis apply to this inquiry? Why should a conclusion about use prevail over what the objects share? Why should a single successful therapy establish exclusive jurisdiction? Why is the request for reasons itself a symptom? The method may answer in its own currency, but it cannot declare the cashier a metaphysical confusion and quietly take the goods home.

I also discovered that I let several declarations wear one coat.

Something common may be a feature, a property, a relation, a convention, an abstraction, a higher-order organization, or an invariant under a prescribed way of comparing things. A necessary condition specifies what every member possesses, while an essence accomplishes something stronger: it explains what renders each member the kind of thing it is. A complete definition specifies membership in both directions of the relation. Those are not prizes awarded at the finishing line of one failed scavenger hunt.

The premise about commonality is the ground floor. Any common essence must include or imply something common, but something common need not be an essence, a complete definition, or even of the first order the Wittgenstein list examines. Rejecting essence cannot reject all forms of commonality.

A minor embarrassment lurks at this junction: should family resemblance characterize every member using one specified set of overlapping similarities, then belonging to that network is an additional common higher-order condition. It may be conventional, relational, trivial, or useless as an essence, but it is common. If no common relation is specified, the description loses the means with which to specify the family, and either way "nothing common" has spent more than the metaphor earned.

Whoever argues against essence need not deny every lower commonality first, but the statement must reach essence. Several visible failed traits do not, and no unpaid invoice can prevent a search through properties, relations, conventions, abstractions, and anything else the original examination never encountered.

Nor does any of this make essence exist as a default. Its presence incurs one debt and its absence another. I have no interest in balancing two camps when only claims have standing. There is no default presence or absence of essence, no default victory for the more fashionable uncertainty.

The trouble was never really with family resemblance, hazy concepts, or the denial of essence, but with premature surrender: allowing a useful warning about one picture of meaning to close an inquiry it has never participated in.

## So I went back to where this started

The Wittgensteinian entry into the discussion of games emerges in a rather qualified manner. Jonne Arjoranta views definitions as a resource for specific discourses: a boundary drawn for the purpose of distinguishing one field from another may have little use in another. Game studies can productively engage the communities, interfaces, labor, fiction, markets, or cultures of games without ever attempting to establish the ontology of Game. [^arjoranta-definitions] “You do not need this definition for your present work” is often the best objection to be launched.

A definition’s point of departure determines its destination, the search for a common measure to classify every game is “a philosophical disease”. Practical unfitness is not a virtue, however, and both Rowe’s objection and his conclusion are spoiled by the very example he gives.

You see it used when someone tries to formulate a definition in their own right. You see it used in a game-design textbook. You see it used when someone wonders where videogames end and toys begin. Family resemblance answers with surprising consistency.

It may be deployed to remind an inquisitor that words are learned by use rather than essential features. It may be used to challenge a candidate by asking them to list what they do and do not include. In both cases, the response demands an answer to which the object of definition is demonstrably unfit.

Sometimes it is simply a convenient summary: a dead philosopher’s pithy summary, with footnotes helpfully removed. Wittgenstein has already been through this particular argument, people have already responded to it, philosophy has since moved on. An objection that has existed for several decades is therefore a counterargument, and a truth claim for which no reply is needed except “but this is no longer a matter for philosophy”.

The fine print is conveniently forgotten. The nature of ordinary usage becomes “the nature of the object”, a failed definition becomes “definition is impossible”, and philosophical therapy quietly turns into ontological conclusion. At this point, it is no longer clear whether the two are even having the same conversation:

OP: I think that the things called games have B

Reply: Family resemblance, games don’t have any feature in common

OP: Does that mean B isn’t true?

Reply: It means games don’t have an essence

OP: B doesn’t have to be an essence! It could be a property, a relation, a convention, an abstraction, a necessary condition

Reply: It wouldn’t define games

OP: I didn’t say it did

Reply: Why are you gatekeeping games?

OP: Is B therefore not true?

Reply: This entire obsession is precisely the problem

Nothing in this exchange has undermined B, the objection has moved from common features to essence, to logical extension, to the morality of gatekeeping, and finally to the very premise of the discussion. The candidate has barely budged from its original position, while everything around it has been swept away.

The effort expended in this objection is notable, the objection itself is clean, concise, self-contained, and could potentially be raised by anyone, anywhere in the discourse. A single counterexample would be sufficient to end the discussion, as would several months’ of patient research. The problem with this objection is not that it fails to address the candidate.

A reply with no counterexamples, no refutation of the premises, no correction of scope fallacies, and no response to the accusations it accidentally answers is simply insufficient to the task of dismissal. The objection has no content beyond the assertion that it has none, the most it can do is demand that B stop being interesting. The most the objector can ask for in return is a concession that B is not wrong.

I would not, thankfully, have needed to write this post. I only needed to confirm that there was no census taken of Reddit’s corners and that the creature described was indeed imaginary. It was easy enough to come up with examples from both game design and philosophy fora, family resemblance can be a productive tool, but it rarely remains one for long. Later replies to the passage in question make its limitations rather obvious. There is no reason to believe that Wittgenstein’s successors could not have identified these limitations, but philosophy had since moved on.

Gatekeeping grants these objections their only potential legitimacy. The suspicion behind them has teeth and a history. “This is not a real game” has been used as an objection to the legitimacy of walking simulators, visual novels, and mobile games, as well as their players and, occasionally, their developers. A predicate about the object quietly functions as an evaluation of its players, then its culture, then its institutional standing. This is the only reason to take these objections seriously, but the logic of the accusation is still inseparable from its consequences.

A chain of implications typically fuels the accusation, but it rarely stops there. Not a game → lesser work → not a work for serious gamers → not made by credible developers → made by someone unworthy of respect. Each link in the chain may have its own objections, but the entire sequence is frequently used as an argument for whichever initial premise it began with. “This is not a game” may be a jumping-off point, but as a conclusion, it requires very little support beyond a willingness to accept all the consequences that follow from its presence.

A category does not logically entail the status of the works it includes, but a community, platform, archive, marketplace, or award can transform categories into gatekeepers. This is not an excuse for doxxing game devs or quietly dismissing entire media categories, but even in the abstract, the position of the accused does not improve their chances against the authority of their critics. The phrase “gatekeeping” names an unfair advantage, but that does not make the advantage any less real.

The debts for these accusations are owed elsewhere. An evaluator owes validity to the object within the scope of its definition. An institution owes responsibility to its audiences for every rule it makes and every penalty it enforces. A critic owes an explanation of where those debts have gone unpaid, and a simple label does not pay them. This is particularly true for the accusations of gatekeeping, which name an unjust power dynamic but rarely examine the practices that reinforce it. “Gatekeeping” announces a set of problematic assumptions, but the details are always left to implication.

## And we are back at the diagnosis

After all that, I return to the word “craving”.

Wittgenstein does not merely identify the failure of generalization as a sign of intellectual disease, he links it to philosophical delusion, scientific pretension, and a condescension toward the particular. The question is compulsory because the imagination is constrained by a picture of the world. Sometimes that is precisely the problem: a demand for a single characteristic is an unwillingness to see the differences, an attempt to impose order on the facts even when no such order exists. A philosopher may waste years defending an improved definition when the real problem was never the definition at all. However, a diagnosis is only as good as its methodology, and one that depends on the presence of symptoms is always vulnerable to failure.

Continued inquiry only confirms this condition. If the subject of discussion really is “the craving for generality”, then the failure of the philosopher is merely a matter of time before they recognize this and move on to their next inquiry. Refusing treatment is another symptom, and the recovery it supposedly brings is only proof of the diagnosis’s precision. The entire enterprise is vulnerable to the same objection, and the only way to dismiss it is to declare the initial statement to have been a meaningless tautology. Even Wittgenstein’s most rigorous critics are unlikely to deny that this sequence could be a legitimate part of his philosophy.

The text has committed itself to philosophy’s role as therapy, it has expanded this commitment to include the entire domain of theory, explanation, and description. It has prohibited the presence of the descriptive from philosophy’s practice, leaving it with one function and one function only: clarifying grammar by separating statements about meaning from everything else. It is hard to say if this limitation is not also a kind of therapy for the reader, but if Wittgenstein wanted to restrict the remit of philosophy, he needed only to clarify the limits of its grammar. The limitations of its methods are not evident in every passage, and it is not clear that Wittgenstein ever meant to discuss them at all. A method’s description of itself may be useful to another method, but it cannot prove its own authority if its existence depends on the validity of every other method in existence.

The practice of linguistic philosophy is self-evidently distinct from that of literary criticism, but science also has little in common with either, and all three are united by their difference from everyday speech. The ability to describe the tools and techniques of other disciplines with greater precision is not a sign of superiority, but a practical skill, no less or more valuable than the ability to recognize one’s own. Redescription is no different, and no less necessary for the practice of philosophy than it is for the rest of the humanities. However, resources are not infinite, and no particular field can devote all of its time and effort to examining the practices of all other fields.

So who is being dogmatic here: the one who demands that a candidate definition be discarded before its premises are ever subjected to scrutiny, or the one who presents a candidate and defends it against all criticism?

What I do not accept is the one-sidedness of such a requirement. An inquiry should have to justify its own authority before the object it wishes to describe before it can proceed to characterize that object. The assertion that the question is misguided may be a convenient evasion, but it has the virtue of modesty. It does not claim to be objective, after all, only to provide grounds for the objection it raises. A candidate that has been demonstrated to be unfit only needs time, patience, and energy to be overthrown, a single statement, however carefully worded, is rarely enough to end the life of a concept.

I do not need the essence to exist, I am not inclined to believe that human experience has one, and I care much more for actual people than for the dignity of the word “game”. I do not need every useful concept to have a clear and precise ordinary-language correlate, I certainly do not need philosophy to imitate physics, or any particular discipline to end its inquiries with the point at which description becomes impossible. I only want each to pay for what it takes, and for what it asks to be taken as true.

Family resemblance is a fitting method, it can describe a practice, break the spell of one particular theory, and remind us that blurred boundaries are not a sign of imprecision. However, these accomplishments are not insignificant, and Wittgenstein’s stronger formulations do not immediately follow from the text’s initial assumptions. If the text has returned the favor, the final word belongs not to family resemblance but to the picture it supposedly replaces. Why should we accept its presence at all?
